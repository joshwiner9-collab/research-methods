The verdict: testing a claim

A sample never matches a claim exactly. Today you learn when a gap is too big to be luck, and how to say so in the words the exam wants.

Five minutes: recall

1. Data 3, 5, 7. What is s, dividing by n?

2. Every value gets +10. What happens to s?

Say both answers out loud before you press the button.

1. Mean 5. Σd² = 4 + 0 + 4 = 8. s² = 8 / 3 = 2.67, s = 1.63.

2. Nothing. Adding a constant moves everything together, so no distance changes.

Guess first

A coffee chain promises an average wait of 4 minutes. You time 16 customers and get an average of 4.6 minutes. Is the promise broken? Answer yes, no, or "I need more information", and say which information.

This is a guess, not a test. No answer is shown. You will know by the end of the page.

1. A trial, not a proof

A test works like a court. H0, the null hypothesis, is the presumption of innocence, and it always holds an equals sign: μ = 4. H1 is the claim someone wants to prove: μ ≠ 4, μ > 4 or μ < 4. Its direction is fixed before anyone looks at the data.

The verdict is either "reject H0" (enough evidence) or "do not reject H0" (not enough). Never "accept H0": a court that lacks evidence says "not guilty", not "innocent".

Two mistakes are possible, and you cannot avoid both at once.

H0 is really trueH0 is really false
Reject H0Type I error (chance α)correct: power = 1 − β
Do not rejectcorrectType II error (chance β)

α, the significance level, is the Type I risk you accept, usually 5%. With the sample size fixed, lowering α makes rejecting harder, so β rises. Only a bigger sample lowers both.

Choosing α is choosing whose mistake you can live with. Drug approval fears Type I (a useless drug on the market), so α is tiny. Airport security fears Type II (a weapon gets through), so it accepts many false alarms. A spam filter fears Type I (a real email, maybe a job offer, buried in spam), so it is kept lenient.

H0 has the "=". H1 is the claim, and its direction is decided before the data. The verdict is "reject" or "do not reject", never "accept".

Worked example

A regulator tests a new drug. H0: the drug does not work. Which error is a Type I here, and which one should the regulator fear more?
1
Say what Type I means in this case.
Rejecting a true H0: approving a drug that does not work.
2
Say what Type II means.
Not rejecting a false H0: blocking a drug that works.
3
Price the two mistakes.
A useless drug reaches millions of patients and may replace real treatment. That is the costly one.
4
Set α.
Fear Type I, so choose a very small α.
Answer: Type I is approving a useless drug. The regulator fears it more and sets α small.
Try it. A researcher wants to lower the chance of both a Type I and a Type II error. What works?
A bigger sample. Changing α only trades one error for the other.
Try it. In a court, H0 is "innocent". What is a Type II error?
Letting a guilty person go: not rejecting H0 when it is false.

2. The t-test for one mean

The test measures the gap between the sample mean and the claim, in units of the standard error, s/√n. The standard error shrinks as n grows: bigger samples wobble less.

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)   ·   df = n − 1

Reading the table: the row is df. A claim with a direction (> or <) reads the top header, one-tail. "Differs" (≠) reads the bottom header, two-tail. If your df is not printed, use the nearest smaller row.

Deciding: two-sided, reject if |t| > t*. One-sided, reject only if t is past t* in the claimed direction; for H1: μ < μ₀, that means below −t*. Software gives a p-value instead: reject if p < α. Same verdict.

The t-test is legal on a random sample, from a roughly bell-shaped population or with n of 30 or more.

Four moves, always in order: write H0 and H1; choose α; compute t and df; compare with the table and write the verdict sentence.

Worked example

The coffee chain promises 4 minutes. n = 16, x̄ = 4.6, s = 1.2. Test at 5%: first "the wait differs from 4", then "the wait is longer than 4".
1
Hypotheses.
H0: μ = 4  ·  H1: μ ≠ 4 (then μ > 4)
2
Standard error.
s / √n = 1.2 / √16 = 1.2 / 4 = 0.3
3
t and df.
t = (4.6 − 4) / 0.3 = 2.00  ·  df = 15
4
Two-sided at 5%.
bottom header 0.05, row 15: 2.131 → |2.00| < 2.131 → do not reject
5
One-sided at 5%.
top header 0.05, row 15: 1.753 → 2.00 > 1.753 → reject
6
Verdict sentences.
Two-sided: "At 5% there is not enough evidence that the mean wait differs from 4 minutes." One-sided: "At 5% there is enough evidence that the mean wait is longer than 4 minutes."
Answer: the same data reject one-sided but not two-sided. That is why the direction must be fixed before the data.
Row 15. The two-sided zones start at ±2.131; the one-sided zone starts at 1.753. t = 2.00 falls between.
Try it. A charity claims families earn less than $1 a day. n = 25, x̄ = 0.92, s = 0.25. Test at 5%.
H1: μ < 1. SE = 0.25 / 5 = 0.05. t = −0.08 / 0.05 = −1.60, df 24. Bar: −1.711. −1.60 is not below it: do not reject. At 10% (−1.318) it would.
Try it. A laptop maker claims 10 hours of battery. Testers suspect less. n = 9, x̄ = 9.4, s = 0.9. At 5%?
SE = 0.9 / 3 = 0.3. t = −0.6 / 0.3 = −2.00, df 8. Bar: −1.860. Reject: enough evidence the battery lasts less than 10 hours.

3. Two samples

To compare two independent groups, the gap between the two means is measured against the standard error of that gap. The two variances add, under one square root.

t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂)   ·   df = n₁ + n₂ − 2
One version of the Lesson 6 slides uses a "conservative" df: the smaller of n₁ − 1 and n₂ − 1. The other version, assignment 6A and both practice exams use n₁ + n₂ − 2, and the practice exams mark "smaller n minus 1" as a wrong answer. Use n₁ + n₂ − 2. If an exam question states its own df rule, follow the question.
Add the squares under one root: s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂. Never add the two standard errors.

Worked example

A firm compares monthly salaries. Men: n = 16, x̄ = 12,800, s = 2,400. Women: n = 16, x̄ = 11,200, s = 2,000. Do the means differ, at 5%?
1
Hypotheses.
H0: μ₁ = μ₂  ·  H1: μ₁ ≠ μ₂
2
Each variance over its n.
2,400² / 16 = 360,000  ·  2,000² / 16 = 250,000
3
Standard error.
√(360,000 + 250,000) = √610,000 = 781.0
4
t and df.
t = 1,600 / 781.0 = 2.05  ·  df = 16 + 16 − 2 = 30
5
Compare.
row 30, two-tail 0.05: 2.042 → 2.05 > 2.042 → reject
6
Verdict.
At 5% there is enough evidence that mean salaries differ. It only just clears the bar.
Answer: t ≈ 2.05, df 30, reject at 5%.
Try it. Same data, but suppose a question told you to use the conservative df, the smaller n − 1 = 15. Verdict?
Row 15, two-tail 0.05: 2.131. 2.05 < 2.131, so do not reject. The df rule changed the verdict, which is why you follow the one the question gives.
Try it. A printout reports p = 0.064 for a two-sided test. Verdict at 5% and at 10%?
At 5%: p > 0.05, do not reject. At 10%: p < 0.10, reject.
The one thing to carry into tomorrow
  • H0 has "="; H1 is the claim, direction fixed first.
  • t = gap / standard error; df = n − 1 or n₁ + n₂ − 2.
  • One-tail header for > or <; two-tail header for ≠.
