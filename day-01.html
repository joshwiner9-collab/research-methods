Variables: what kind of data is this?

Every tool in this course depends on one first decision: what kind of variable you are looking at. Today you learn to make it without thinking.

This is the first day, so there is nothing to recall yet. Tomorrow starts with two questions on today.

This is a guess, not a test. No answer is shown. You will know by the end of the page.

1. Why a good story is not evidence

The lecturer opens with a question: can you build a career without a degree? Everyone knows the answer "Bill Gates dropped out". That is an anecdote, one story used as if it were proof.

Stories fail for four separate reasons. Each one has a name, and the exam uses the names.

ReasonWhat goes wrong
Too few observationsOne cousin is one data point. One case cannot show a pattern.
Survivorship (selection) biasYou only hear about the winners. The dropout who drives a taxi is never interviewed.
Confirmation biasYou remember the stories that fit what you already believe.
InaccuracyStories change each time they are retold. Numbers grow.

Statistics replaces the story with a process: ask a question, design the study, collect data, describe it, infer, decide. The words that matter most:

Population: everyone the question is about. Sample: the part you actually measured. A number that describes the population is a parameter. The same number computed from the sample is a statistic. Inference is the bridge from the sample back to the population, and it only works if the sample is random and representative.

Before you believe a number, ask two questions: who was measured, and who was left out?

Worked example

A "study" finds that 98% of convicted offenders eat bread. The authors call for a bread tax. What is wrong with the finding?
1
Say what is being compared.
The claim compares offenders to nothing. It gives one group only.
2
Find the base rate.
About 98% of everyone eats bread. That is the base rate: how common the trait is in the whole population.
3
Compare the two numbers.
offenders: about 98% eat bread  ·  everyone: about 98% eat bread
The two are the same, so bread tells you nothing about crime.
4
Name the trick.
This is base-rate neglect: quoting a share for one group without the share for everyone else.
Answer: base-rate neglect. The fix is to compare offenders with non-offenders.
Try it. The same "study" says tribes that never ate bread show almost no Alzheimer's disease. What is the trick?
Confounding. Alzheimer's is a disease of old age, and people in those tribes have a short life expectancy. A third factor, how long people live, explains the gap. Bread has nothing to do with it.
Try it. A poll of 1,200 voters finds 34% support party X. Is 34% a parameter or a statistic?
A statistic. It comes from the sample of 1,200. The parameter is the true share among all voters, which nobody observes directly.

2. Qualitative or quantitative, and which kind

Every variable gets a label for its nature: what kind of values it takes.

TypeValues are…Examples
Qualitative, nominalnames with no ordercity, brand, blood type, shirt number
Qualitative, ordinalcategories with an order, but no fixed distancesmilitary rank, credit rating AAA to BBB, satisfaction 1 to 5
Quantitative, discretecounts: 0, 1, 2, …children in a family, complaints per month, calendar year
Quantitative, continuousmeasurements: any value in between is possibleheight, time, temperature, price, market share

The trap is digits. A number is not always a quantity. Player 10 is not "twice" player 5. Coding private = 1, corporal = 2, sergeant = 3 does not turn rank into a number, because "corporal minus private" is not 1 of anything.

Two honest details from the slides. Age is continuous even though we report whole years: the rounding is ours. Money is technically discrete (agorot exist), but we treat salaries and prices as continuous because the steps are tiny.

The test: does an average of these values mean anything? If not, the variable is qualitative, whatever it looks like. If it does, ask: is it counted (discrete) or measured (continuous)?

Worked example

The Lesson 1 questionnaire asks you to classify "IDF rank coded as a number: private = 1, corporal = 2, sergeant = 3".
1
Apply the average test.
The average of 1, 1, 3 is 1.67. There is no rank called 1.67, so the average means nothing.
2
So it is qualitative.
Qualitative, whatever the digits suggest.
3
Is there an order?
Yes: sergeant is above corporal, which is above private. So it is ordinal.
4
Check against the uncoded version.
"IDF rank (private, corporal, sergeant)" gets the same answer. Coding changes the labels, not the nature.
Answer: qualitative, ordinal.

3. Who explains whom, and what each type allows

The second label is the variable's role in the question. The dependent variable is the thing being explained (sales, salary, exam score). The independent variable does the explaining (ad spend, education, hours studied). Roles are not tattoos: salary is dependent when you study pay gaps, and independent when you study job satisfaction.

The type also decides which "centre" you are allowed to use. You meet these properly on day 3, but the rule starts here.

TypeModeMedianMean
Nominalyesnono
Ordinalyesyesrisky
Quantitativeyesyesyes
Two variables moving together does not show which one causes the other. Before saying "causes", look for a third factor that could drive both, and ask whether the arrow could point the other way.

Worked example

"Economics students who submitted all the exercises got a higher score on the statistics exam." Name the dependent and independent variables, classify each, and say whether the exercises caused the higher scores.
1
Find what is being explained.
The exam score. It is the dependent variable, and it is quantitative.
2
Find what explains it.
Submitted all exercises: yes or no. It is the independent variable, and it is qualitative (two categories), even in a numbers course.
3
Look for a third factor.
Diligent students both submit exercises and study more. Diligence could drive both.
4
Decide about cause.
The link is real. The causal claim is not proven, because students chose themselves into the "submitted" group.
Answer: score is dependent (quantitative); submitting is independent (qualitative); association, not proven cause.
Try it. "Does the central bank interest rate affect the number of mortgage applications?" Name and classify both variables.
Dependent: number of mortgage applications, quantitative discrete (a count). Independent: interest rate, quantitative continuous.
Try it. "Does advertising drive monthly sales?" The data show ads and sales moving together. Why can the arrow point both ways?
Reverse causality. Ads may raise sales, but a firm with high sales also has more money to spend on ads. Both stories fit the same data.
The one thing to carry into tomorrow
  • Anecdotes fail for four named reasons. Statistics compares groups.
  • Average test first: meaningless average means qualitative.
  • Moving together is not causing. Look for the third factor.
