Tables and pictures

Fifty raw scores answer nothing. One well-built table answers almost everything. Today: how to build it, and which picture is legal for which variable.

Five minutes: recall

1. What type of variable is a football shirt number, and why?

2. A survey of 500 customers finds an average spend of 212 NIS. Is 212 a parameter or a statistic?

Say both answers out loud before you press the button.

1. Qualitative, nominal. The number is a label: player 10 is not "twice" player 5, and the average of shirt numbers means nothing.

2. A statistic. It comes from the sample of 500. The parameter is the true average of all customers.

Guess first

Forty students took a quantum-theory exam. The class 70 to 75 holds 10 students. The class 85 to 100 also holds 10 students. Which stretch of scores is more crowded, or are they equal?

This is a guess, not a test. No answer is shown. You will know by the end of the page.

1. The frequency table: three columns

A frequency table says how often each value appears. It has up to three columns, and each has a job.

ColumnFormulaWhat it tells you
f(x), frequencycount the caseshow many have value x
p(x), relative frequencyf(x) / nthe share, so two samples of different size can be compared
F(x), cumulativerunning total of fhow many have a value at most x

The cumulative column only makes sense when the values have an order. "Up to Facebook" means nothing, so a nominal variable gets f and p only. An ordered variable, like rank or satisfaction 1 to 5, can have F.

Two sanity checks on every table: Σf = n, and Σp = 100%.

"At most x" is F(x). "At least x" is n minus F of the value just below x. "Fewer than x" means at most the value below x.

Worked example

A sample of 10 families records the number of people in each: 2, 2, 6, 5, 3, 5, 5, 4, 3, 2. Build the table. How many families have at most 3 people? How many have at least 3?
1
Sort and count.
xf(x)p(x)F(x)
2330%3
3220%5
4110%6
5330%9
6110%10
2
Check the table.
Σf = 3 + 2 + 1 + 3 + 1 = 10 = n  ·  Σp = 100%
3
At most 3.
F(3) = 5 families
4
At least 3: subtract the families below 3.
n − F(2) = 10 − 3 = 7 families
5
Notice the overlap.
5 + 7 = 12, which is more than 10. That is correct: families of exactly 3 are counted in both answers.
Answer: 5 families have at most 3 people; 7 have at least 3.
Try it. In the same families data, how many families have fewer than 4 people?
"Fewer than 4" means at most 3. F(3) = 5.
Try it. 73 students say which social network they would keep: Instagram 43, Facebook 16, Twitter 4, TikTok 2, LinkedIn 1, none 7. What is p(Facebook), and should this table have an F column?
p = 16 / 73 = 21.9%. No F column: the networks have no order, so a running total means nothing.

2. Classes, width and density

When a variable has many values (heights, times, exam scores), you group them into classes. In this course a class like 150 to 155 includes 150 and excludes 155, so every value lands in exactly one class.

Grouping costs something: you lose the exact values. The working assumption is that cases are spread evenly inside each class.

Each class has a width ℓ = upper − lower, and a density d = f / ℓ: how many cases per one unit of the variable. 9 students in a 5 cm class is 1.8 students per cm.

In a histogram the bar height is the density d, not f. Then the area of each bar, ℓ × d, equals f, and the total area equals n. The most crowded class is the one with the highest d.

Worked example

Final scores of 40 students: 40–60: 5, 60–70: 5, 70–75: 10, 75–85: 10, 85–100: 10. Which class is the most crowded?
1
Find each width.
ℓ = 20, 10, 5, 10, 15
2
Divide f by ℓ.
d = 5/20 = 0.25 · 5/10 = 0.5 · 10/5 = 2 · 10/10 = 1 · 10/15 = 0.67
3
Pick the highest d.
70–75 has d = 2: on average 2 students on every single grade point.
4
Check the areas.
20·0.25 + 10·0.5 + 5·2 + 10·1 + 15·0.67 = 5 + 5 + 10 + 10 + 10 = 40 = n
Answer: 70–75 is the most crowded, even though three classes tie on f = 10.
The correct histogram: heights are densities. The tall narrow bar is the crowded stretch.
The same data drawn with heights = p(x). The wide 85–100 and 40–60 blocks look huge because they are wide, not because they are crowded.
Try it. Heights of 72 students: the class 175–180 cm holds 16. What is its density?
d = 16 / 5 = 3.2 students per cm. All classes in that table are 5 cm wide, so the largest f and the largest d pick the same class.
Try it. Manhattan, 24 October days: 5–8 °C: 4, 8–11: 8, 11–14: 6, 14–17: 5, 17–20: 1. On how many days was it at least 11 °C?
F up to 11 is 4 + 8 = 12. n − 12 = 12 days.

3. The cumulative graph, and which chart is legal

The cumulative graph (the ogive) plots F at the upper bound of each class, starts at zero on the lower bound of the first class, and joins the points with straight lines. The lines are straight because we assumed an even spread inside each class. The ogive can never go down. A steep part means a crowded stretch.

VariableLegal pictures
Qualitative (nominal or ordinal)Bar chart. A pie only for a few parts of a whole.
Quantitative, discreteBar chart with gaps, plus a frequency polygon
Quantitative, continuous or groupedHistogram with density heights, polygon, ogive
Anything over timeLine chart
A bar chart shows value as length, so the axis must start at zero. Cut the base and a small gap looks huge. 3-D pies make the front slice look bigger than it is.

Worked example

For the 40 exam scores above, F = 5 at 60, 10 at 70, 20 at 75, 30 at 85, 40 at 100. How many scored below 75? What share scored 70 or more?
1
Below 75: read F at 75.
F(75) = 20 students
2
70 or more: subtract those below 70.
n − F(70) = 40 − 10 = 30 students
3
Turn it into a share.
30 / 40 = 75%
4
Read the meaning.
Exactly half the class is below 75, so 75 is the halfway score. On day 3 that gets a name: the median.
Answer: 20 scored below 75; 75% scored 70 or more.
Try it. A chart shows Brand A at 82 and Brand B at 79, with the axis starting at 78. How many times taller does A's bar look?
Only the part above 78 is drawn: 4 against 1, so A looks 4 times taller. The real gap is 3 points out of about 80.
The one thing to carry into tomorrow
  • At most x is F(x). At least x is n minus F one step below.
  • Histogram height is density, d = f / ℓ.
  • Bars start at zero, always.
