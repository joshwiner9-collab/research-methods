Two variables together: covariance and r

Do two things move together, and how tightly? Look at the picture first, then let one number sum it up, and learn the three ways that number can fool you.

Five minutes: recall

1. A quiz has mean 40 and s 6. What is the z of a score of 31?

2. Why is the CV useless for temperatures in °C?

Say both answers out loud before you press the button.

1. (31 − 40) / 6 = −1.5: one and a half standard deviations below the mean.

2. The zero is arbitrary. The mean can be near zero or negative, so s / mean explodes or flips sign without the spread changing.

Guess first

Across the months of a year, ice-cream sales and drownings move together very strongly. A councillor wants to ban ice cream at the beach. Would it save lives? One line on why or why not.

This is a guess, not a test. No answer is shown. You will know by the end of the page.

1. The scatter plot and the covariance

Always draw first. A scatter plot puts one dot per case: x across, y up. Only the eye sees curves, clusters and single wild points.

Now draw a cross through the point of means (x̄, ȳ). It cuts the plot into four rooms. Dots in the top-right and bottom-left rooms have both deviations with the same sign: their product dₓ·dᵧ is positive. Dots in the other two rooms give negative products. The covariance averages those products.

cov(x, y) = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / n

Positive: they rise together. Negative: one rises as the other falls. Near zero: no straight-line link. Only the sign can be read. The size carries units like "shekels times days", so 6 or 6,000 tells you nothing on its own.

Read the sign of a covariance, never its size. Rows whose product is zero still count in n.

Worked example

A retailer records five weeks: ad spend x = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and sales y = 5, 4, 8, 7, 11 (both thousand NIS). Find the covariance.

Means.
x̄ = 30 / 5 = 6  ·  ȳ = 35 / 5 = 7
2
Deviations.

3
Divide by n.
cov = 30 / 5 = +6

Read the sign.
Positive: weeks with above-average ads tend to have above-average sales. The two zero rows still count in n = 5.
Answer: cov = +6. The sign speaks; the 6 does not.
The same five weeks. Most dots sit in the top-right and bottom-left rooms around (x̄, ȳ), so the products are mostly positive.
Try it. Finance re-enters the sales in NIS instead of thousands. What happens to the covariance?
Every dᵧ is multiplied by 1,000, so the covariance becomes 6,000. Nothing real changed. That is why the size cannot be read.
Try it. A pair of variables gives products dₓ·dᵧ that sum to −45 over 9 cases. What does the sign say?
cov = −45 / 9 = −5, negative: when one is above its mean, the other tends to be below its mean.

2. Pearson's r: the covariance with the units removed

Divide the covariance by both standard deviations and the units cancel. What is left always sits between −1 and +1.

r = cov(x, y) / (sₓ · sᵧ)
|r|Reading
0 to 0.10negligible
0.10 to 0.39weak
0.40 to 0.69medium
0.70 to 0.89strong
0.90 to 1very strong

Three guard rails. One: r only hears straight lines. A perfect U-shape can score r = 0. Two: r is built from means and standard deviations, so a single wild point can create or destroy it. Three: moving together is not causing. Name the third factor before naming a cause.

Also: r ignores steepness. A steep perfect line and a shallow perfect line both score ±1. How much y moves per step is the slope's job, on day 8.

Sign = direction, size = strength. r = 0 means no straight-line link, not no link. Draw before you trust it.

Worked example

Hours studied x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and grades y = 60, 65, 75, 70, 80 (the slides' class drill). Find cov and r.
1
Means.
x̄ = 3  ·  ȳ = 350 / 5 = 70
2
Products.
dₓ: −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 · dᵧ: −10, −5, 5, 0, 10 · products: 20, 5, 0, 0, 20 → Σ = 45
3
Covariance.
45 / 5 = 9
4
Standard deviations.
sₓ = √(10/5) = √2 = 1.414  ·  sᵧ = √(250/5) = √50 = 7.071
5
r.
9 / (1.414 × 7.071) = 9 / 10 = 0.90
6
Ladder.
0.90 is on the edge of very strong: one wiggle (the 4-hour student) away from a straight line.
Answer: cov = 9, r = 0.90.
Try it. Across 10 ordinary stores r(ad spend, sales) = 0.15. Adding the flagship, with ten times the ads and sales of any other, makes r = 0.80. Which number should the CEO hear?
0.15 for the ten comparable stores, with the flagship treated separately. One point dominated both means and both standard deviations and manufactured the correlation.
Try it. In a drug study r = 0.02 between dose and benefit. What must be checked before writing "no relationship"?
The scatter plot. Benefit that rises and then falls (a therapeutic window) gives r near 0 even though the link is real.

3. The portfolio law: why negative covariance is a free lunch

Covariance earns its salary in finance. The risk of a mix of two assets depends on how they move together.

var(w·x + (1 − w)·y) = w²·var(x) + (1 − w)²·var(y) + 2·w·(1 − w)·cov(x, y)

The first two terms are always positive. The third one has the sign of the covariance. If the two assets tend to move in opposite directions, it subtracts risk: one's bad year is the other's good year.

Square the weights. Remember the 2 in front of the covariance term. A negative covariance lowers risk without lowering the expected return.

Worked example

Two stocks over five years. X: 10, −2, 6, 14, 2 (%). Y: 5, 10, 9, 1, 5 (%). An investor puts half in each. Find the portfolio's expected return and risk.
1
Means.
x̄ = 30 / 5 = 6%  ·  ȳ = 30 / 5 = 6%
2
Covariance.
products: −4, −32, 0, −40, +4 → Σ = −72 → cov = −72 / 5 = −14.4
3
Variances.
var(x) = 160 / 5 = 32 (s 5.66%) · var(y) = 52 / 5 = 10.4 (s 3.22%)
4
Expected return.
½·6 + ½·6 = 6%
5
Portfolio variance.
¼·32 + ¼·10.4 + 2·½·½·(−14.4) = 8 + 2.6 − 7.2 = 3.4
6
Risk.
σ = √3.4 = 1.84%
7
Compare.
The average of the two risks is (5.66 + 3.22) / 2 = 4.44%. The risk of the mix is 1.84%.
Answer: same 6% return, less than half the risk. The whole discount is the −7.2 covariance term.
Try it. Same stocks, but suppose cov had been +14.4. What is the portfolio risk now?
8 + 2.6 + 7.2 = 17.8, σ = √17.8 = 4.22%: almost the plain average. Assets that move together are the same bet twice.
Try it. The slides' war stock C (σ 31%) and peace stock D (σ 82%) have cov −0.103. A 50/50 mix has risk of about what?
¼·0.094 + ¼·0.675 − ½·0.103 = 0.141. √0.141 = 37.5%, against an average risk of 56.5%.
The one thing to carry into tomorrow
  • Draw first. Covariance: sign only.
  • r = cov / (sₓ·sᵧ), between −1 and +1; read it on the ladder.
  • Negative covariance subtracts portfolio risk.
