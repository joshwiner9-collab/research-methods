Where do you stand? z-scores and the CV

Your 85 or your friend's 90: raw numbers cannot say who did better. Divide by the right thing and they can.

Five minutes: recall

1. Wages are right-skewed. Put mode, median and mean in order.

2. 30 calls average 6 minutes and 20 calls average 11. Overall mean?

Say both answers out loud before you press the button.

1. mode < median < mean. The mean chases the long right tail.

2. (30·6 + 20·11) / 50 = 400 / 50 = 8 minutes. Not 8.5: weight by group size.

Guess first

You scored 85 on a brutal exam. A friend scored 90 on an easy one. Name the two extra numbers you would need about each exam to know who really did better.

This is a guess, not a test. No answer is shown. You will know by the end of the page.

1. The CV: comparing spreads fairly

A standard deviation speaks the variable's own language: cm, kg, shekels. "Is 14 cm of spread more than 9 kg of spread?" does not even make sense. The slides call this the units prison, and division is the way out.

The coefficient of variation divides s by the mean. The units cancel, and what is left is the wobble per unit of typical value, usually quoted as a percentage.

CV = s / x̄

Low CV means homogeneous: the data hug their own average. High CV means scattered. The CV needs a meaningful mean: use it for positive values measured from a true zero, such as money, time and weight. Not for °C, and not for rating codes.

To compare the spread of two datasets in different units, or with very different means, compare CVs, not standard deviations.

Worked example

A class has mean height 174 cm (s = 14 cm) and mean weight 60 kg (s = 9 kg). Is the class more alike in height or in weight?
1
The lazy answer.
"14 > 9, so height is more spread." Those are cm against kg, so the comparison is meaningless.
2
CV of height.
14 / 174 = 0.080 = 8%
3
CV of weight.
9 / 60 = 0.15 = 15%
4
Compare.
Relative to its own average, weight is nearly twice as dispersed as height.
Answer: the class is far more alike in height. The raw comparison pointed the wrong way.
Try it. Dr. Havatzelet's second clinic: mean 1.86 cups, s = 1.12 cups. What is the CV, and what does it say?
CV = 1.12 / 1.86 = 0.60 = 60%. A very scattered crowd: the typical wobble is 60% of the average itself.
Try it. Fund A earns 12% a year with s = 20%. Fund B earns 6% with s = 5%. Which carries less risk per unit of return?
CV A = 20 / 12 = 1.67. CV B = 5 / 6 = 0.83. Fund B carries half the risk per unit of return.

2. The z-score: you against your crowd

The CV compares whole datasets. The z-score places one observation inside its own crowd. The top is your distance from the mean; the bottom is how far is normal here.

z = (x − x̄) / s   ·   back again: x = x̄ + z · s

z counts standard deviations. z = +2 is two of the crowd's s above its mean. z = −0.5 is half an s below. z has no units, so any z can be compared with any other z.

The reverse gear, x = x̄ + z·s, rebuilds the raw value: start at the mean and walk z steps of size s.

Standardize each result against its own group's mean and s. Then compare the z's, not the raw numbers.

Worked example

A student got 85 in Statistics (class mean 82, s 5) and 80 in Intro to Micro (class mean 70, s 3). Which result is stronger?
1
z for Statistics.
(85 − 82) / 5 = 3 / 5 = 0.60
2
z for Micro.
(80 − 70) / 3 = 10 / 3 = 3.33
3
Compare.
3.33 standard deviations above the class, against 0.60.
Answer: the 80 in Micro is far stronger. The raw 85 hides a merely good result.
Same z-axis, two raw scales riding on top. That is what "standardized" means.
Try it. Class heights have mean 165 cm (s 10) and weights mean 60 kg (s 12). Roy has z = 2 in both. What are his height and weight?
Height = 165 + 2·10 = 185 cm. Weight = 60 + 2·12 = 84 kg. Same z, different distances moved, because the rulers differ.
Try it. IQ in that class: mean 100, s 12. Iti scored 130. What is his z, and did you need it to know he had the top IQ of the class trio (90, 130, 100)?
z = (130 − 100) / 12 = 2.5. You did not need z to rank them: same trait, same crowd, so raw scores rank directly. z adds how exceptional.

3. How big is big, and what never moves z

Standardize any dataset and it always lands on mean 0 and s 1: the deviations sum to zero, and dividing Σd²/n by s² gives exactly 1. That is why z is the common currency of every comparison.

|z|Reading
under 1the crowd: ordinary
1 to 2notable
2 to 3rare: worth a close look
over 3alarm: exceptional talent, a data error, or a different population

How much data can sit far out? It depends on what you know about the shape.

Any shape (Chebyshev): at least 1 − 1/k² of the data lie within k standard deviations. Put the other way: at most 1/k² lie beyond. Beyond 2: at most 25%. Beyond 3: at most 11%.

Bell shape only: about 68% within 1 s, 95% within 2 s (1.960 exactly), 99.7% within 3 s. 90% lie within 1.645 s, so the top 5% start at x̄ + 1.645·s.

The 68-95-99.7 rule. It only holds when the data are shown to be bell-shaped.
z survives any change applied to everyone: adding a constant or multiplying by a positive number. Raises move salaries, not standings.

Worked example

A factory's salaries average 5,700 NIS with s = 700. Daniela has z = 2.5. Everyone gets a 7% raise. Does her z go up?
1
Her salary before.
5,700 + 2.5 × 700 = 7,450
2
Everything is multiplied by 1.07.
Daniela 7,450 × 1.07 = 7,971.5 · mean 5,700 × 1.07 = 6,099 · s 700 × 1.07 = 749
3
New z.
(7,971.5 − 6,099) / 749 = 1,872.5 / 749 = 2.5
4
Why.
The 1.07 multiplies the top and the bottom of the fraction and cancels.
Answer: FALSE that it rises. Her z stays exactly 2.5.
Try it. "In a left-skewed distribution, the standard score of the fifth decile is negative." True or false?
FALSE. The fifth decile is the median. Left skew pulls the mean below the median, so median − mean is positive, and so is its z.
Try it. Shape unknown. At most what share of values can lie more than 3 standard deviations from the mean?
At most 1/3² = 1/9 ≈ 11%. The 0.3% figure needs a bell shape.
The one thing to carry into tomorrow
  • CV = s / x̄ compares datasets; z = (x − x̄)/s places one value.
  • x = x̄ + z·s rebuilds the value.
  • Chebyshev for any shape; 68-95-99.7 only for bells.
